Research

Dissertation

Cyber, Signal, Satellite, Spy: Intelligence Collection and Stability in Nuclear Rivalries

My dissertation examines how the ways states spy on one another affect the risk of arms races, crises, and war. International relations scholars have long held that information about rivals’ capabilities stabilizes competition, but they have paid little attention to how states acquire that information. I argue that collection technologies shape the risks and trade-offs of spying through two characteristics. Methods with normative cover, meaning plausible protection under international norms such as freedom of navigation or freedom of use of outer space, tend to be tolerated by their targets, while methods that violate sovereign spaces risk provoking crises. Methods with low vulnerability to countermeasures allow leaders to reveal what they have learned without enabling targets to nullify the collection.

When both conditions hold, leaders can leverage intelligence, turning private knowledge into shared information by confronting rivals with evidence of their capabilities, verifying arms control agreements unilaterally, and rebutting inflated threat claims at home.

I test the theory with an original dataset of all 22 methods great powers have used to collect on strategic forces since 1945, spanning domains from sea to cyberspace. The dataset reveals what I call the remote sensing revolution: the development of stand-off methods such as satellite reconnaissance, seismic detection of nuclear tests, and space surveillance, which combined normative cover with low vulnerability to countermeasures and helped make Cold War arms control possible. Case studies of aerial overflights, satellite reconnaissance, and telemetry interception trace the causal mechanisms, drawing on declassified records from the Eisenhower, Kennedy, Johnson, Ford, and Carter presidential libraries, the Library of Congress, the Central Intelligence Agency, and the National Reconnaissance Office. I am developing the dissertation into a book manuscript, which will add a chapter on space situational awareness.

Committee: Jack Snyder (chair), Erica Lonergan, Allison Carnegie, Elizabeth Saunders

Working Papers

Strategic Intelligence: Collection Technologies and the Risks and Uses of Spying in Nuclear Rivalries

This is my job market paper. It develops the dissertation’s theory of how collection technologies create trade-offs between provoking targets and gathering intelligence, and between disclosing what states know and protecting their sources.

Deterrence Up the Ladder? Second-Strike Counterforce Doctrine and Strategic Stability

This project examines the understudied concept of second-strike counterforce in U.S. nuclear strategy. I argue that U.S. strategists favored retaliatory counterforce because they recognized the political constraints on first-strike strategies, and that improved communications and reconnaissance made it increasingly feasible, culminating in the Carter administration’s nuclear strategy.

Other Projects

A Second Sensing Revolution? Emerging Collection Technologies and Strategic Stability

This project asks whether new collection technologies, including proliferated commercial remote sensing constellations, space-based space situational awareness, AI-enabled analysis and data fusion, and uncrewed aerial and undersea vehicles, amount to a second sensing revolution, and whether it will stabilize great power competition as the first one did. It assesses the normative cover and vulnerability to countermeasures of each emerging method against the historical record, and examines recent disclosures and provocations such as the 2024 disclosure of Russia’s development of a nuclear antisatellite weapon.

Artificial Intelligence, Space-based Intelligence, Surveillance, and Reconnaissance, and Strategic Stability

This project investigates how proliferated space-based intelligence, surveillance, and reconnaissance (ISR) constellations and artificial intelligence capabilities for analyzing ISR data affect strategic stability, including arms control verification, strategic warning, and the vulnerability of nuclear forces. In “Precarious Transparency: Artificial Intelligence, Space-based ISR Capabilities, and US-China Strategic Stability,” co-authored with Ted Fujimoto and published by the Council on Strategic Risks and the Berkeley Risk and Security Lab, we argue that AI will produce contingent, limited increases in the vulnerability of forces like mobile missiles, depending on whether AI systems can adapt to chaotic wartime conditions and on the use of counterspace capabilities.
